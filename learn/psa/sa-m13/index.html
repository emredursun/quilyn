The Approval Step

Approval Step

An approval routes a Case to a user (the approver) to either APPROVE or REJECT the work. It enhances business reliability and accountability by requiring sign-off within the Process before the Case proceeds.

An approval process in Pega is like a document traveling through an office for wet-ink signatures: each approver sees only their signature page, signs or rejects it, and passes it on automatically. The difference from paper: Pega routes the document to the next approver instantly, logs who signed when with a timestamp, and escalates via SLA if someone leaves the form sitting in their inbox too long.

Approve vs. reject routing

The approver's outcome determines the routing path:

  • APPROVED — typically continues the primary path (Case moves forward)
  • REJECTED — branches to rework, a rejection Stage, or another defined path (configure what happens for each)

Three approval actions

At an Approval Assignment, the approver can: APPROVE (work meets requirements — continue), REJECT (does not meet requirements — follow rejection path), or SEND BACK (return to submitter for clarification, then re-route for decision). 'Send Back' is most often missed.

No response → add an SLA

If an approver may not respond in time, attach a Service-Level Agreement (SLA) to the approval Assignment. The SLA timer can escalate, reassign, or even auto-approve/auto-advance the Case after the deadline, so work does not stall waiting on one approver.

Configuring a single approval

Single approval

A single approval requires ONE approver. You specify who approves (e.g., a specific user, the manager of the current operator, or a user determined by business logic) and the routing for approve/reject outcomes.

Choosing the approver

The approver can be routed like any Assignment — to a specific user, a manager (reporting relationship), a Work Queue, or by business logic — so the right person signs off.

Single vs. Sequential vs. Cascading

Approvals scale three ways:

  • SINGLE = one approver
  • SEQUENTIAL = a fixed chain (each must act before the next)
  • CASCADING = derived from an authority matrix, where the number of levels depends on threshold rules
